The plan

What we will ship,
in order.

  1. Freeze the endpoint spec, v1. One OpenAI-compatible surface at localhost:1134/v1 plus discovery — published, versioned, boring on purpose.
  2. Ship the SDKs. TypeScript, Python, Swift, Kotlin — each with a five-line quickstart and a reference app.
  3. Ship the MCP server. Claude Code, agents and IDEs see the mesh as tools on day one.
  4. Release the plug-in pack. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and the agent stacks — one install page, each kept current by a named member.
  5. Maintain the forks. LibreChat first, then the next most-used open clients — thin forks, loud upstream PRs.
  6. Open Mesh Ready certification. A public test suite; pass it and your client carries the badge. The registry lists every certified client.

Nothing above asks permission from a platform. The endpoint is yours, on your hardware — clients are just doors into it. The more doors, the less any landlord matters.

The Context Vault — your memory, staying home.

Every assistant you have ever used remembers you on someone else’s server — and forgets you the day you switch. The vault inverts that: one small encrypted store on your device that any registry model can consult, and no company can. Switch models, switch clients, keep your life.

The Context Vault — the concept, in full

The problem it solves

Personal context is the moat every AI company is racing to dig: the more an assistant remembers about you, the harder it is to leave. That memory lives on their servers, under their terms, readable by their systems and subpoenaable by anyone with leverage over them. The lock-in is the product. A public-good client has to break exactly this — not with a policy, with an architecture.

The store

One SQLite file plus a vector index, in your home directory. Entries are typed — a note, a person, a preference, a document pointer — each with a source, a timestamp and a scope tag. A phone-class embedding model from the registry maintains the index on-device, re-embedding in the background when the model revision changes. Total footprint for a heavy user: hundreds of megabytes, not gigabytes.

The seal

The store is encrypted at rest with XChaCha20-Poly1305; the key never leaves the device secure enclave (or a passphrase-derived key where no enclave exists). Unlock happens at session start, the way a password manager does it. A stolen laptop yields a blob.

The grants

Apps and clients see nothing by default. Each one requests scopes — notes, calendar, people, health — and the grant is per-app, logged in a local audit trail you can read, and revocable in one tap. A revoked app keeps nothing: it never held the data, only the right to ask.

The retrieval path

When a model answers you, the gateway runs retrieval locally: the query is embedded on-device, the top excerpts are pulled from the vault and placed into the prompt, and the prompt goes to whichever model you chose — on your machine or on your org’s mesh node. The model sees excerpts. The vault, as a whole, is never uploaded, synced or mirrored. There is no server copy to breach, sell or subpoena.

The handoff

New phone, new laptop: export produces one encrypted file, moved over the mesh or a cable, imported with your key. Leaving Enki entirely is the same export — the format is documented, so your memory is portable even away from us. Lock-in that survives an open spec was never lock-in worth having.

Status: concept. The vault is a design the client team is prototyping — the store format and grant model will ship as a draft spec in the open repository before any build claims the name.
