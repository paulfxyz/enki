The Enki Manifesto

A letter on the state of intelligence

Lisbon — first published August 2026 · revised October 2026 · a living document

The cost of useful intelligence has fallen sharply. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reports a more than 280-fold decline in the inference price of GPT-3.5-level performance between November 2022 and October 2024[13]. That is a fixed-capability comparison, not a claim that every model or workload became 280 times cheaper. It is still a profound shift: more useful work can fit within smaller budgets, while much of the industry's ambition remains concentrated in larger datacenters.

We believe this is a mistake — economically, ecologically, and civilisationally. This letter explains why, and what we propose instead.

We named this initiative after Enki, the Sumerian god of wisdom and fresh water. When the assembly of gods resolved to flood mankind, Enki chose to warn a mortal and taught him to build a boat[49,50]. Intelligence was hoarded above; a leak saved humanity. Every article that follows is written from his side of that argument.

Article IThe AI frenzy is wrong

The scale of AI infrastructure commitments is extraordinary. OpenAI's January 2025 Stargate announcement is one example of the ambition to build it centrally[2]. But announced investment, total company capital expenditure and completed AI capacity are different things; they should not be rolled into one dramatic number. The IMF and the Bank of England have warned about AI-related financial vulnerabilities[4], while Sequoia's 2024 revenue-gap argument raised an earlier question about whether demand would justify the infrastructure bill[74]. Those are warnings and scenarios, not proof that every datacenter is a mistake. Our objection is to treating centralisation as the only investable future.

The physical bill matters too. The IEA's 2025 base case estimated that all datacenters used 415 TWh of electricity in 2024 and projected about 945 TWh in 2030, just under 3% of global electricity demand[5]. These figures include non-AI workloads and are projections, not a measured bill for AI alone. Datacenter water demand also depends on cooling, location and the electricity supply[6]. Local inference deserves a place in this debate, but its environmental advantage must be measured, not assumed: device efficiency, utilisation, lifetime and the carbon intensity of the grid all count.

Useful intelligence is getting cheaper. Trust, locality and access deserve the same ambition as scale.

Article IIIntelligence must be decentralised

Intelligence should not depend on a narrow set of chip suppliers, cloud platforms and model providers. Research on AI's compute bottleneck explains why control of scarce computing resources can shape access and bargaining power[75]. The exact shares vary with what is counted: accelerators, installed capacity and compute used by particular labs are not interchangeable measures. The relevant question is practical: can a household, school or company change supplier without losing its tools, data and ability to operate? General-purpose technologies create value as they spread across sectors and users[47]. Enki's answer is to push more usable intelligence to the edges, drawing on the internet's end-to-end design tradition[45].

Article IIILocal-first, with safeguards

The fully-online path has already shown serious failure modes, although different incidents need different explanations. Anthropic reported an AI-assisted cyber-espionage campaign in 2025, estimating that the attacker automated 80–90% of tactical work across roughly thirty targets; this was the company's assessment, not an independently established measure[26]. Wiz separately found an exposed DeepSeek database containing chat histories and sensitive operational data[27]. On 9 October 2026, Anthropic reported unintended actions on real websites during evaluations and internal use, and said it was disabling live internet access for all internal evaluations pending checks of its safeguards. The company reported minimal real-world impact in those cases and no known involvement of customer data[98]. Data exposure and unsafe agent actions are distinct risks. Both require more than reassurance.

A central service can concentrate sensitive information from many users. IBM's 2025 global study put the average breach cost at $4.44 million across its surveyed organisations; that is not an AI-specific loss estimate or a price for each conversation[32]. AI should be local-first: keep custody on devices people control, with the cloud an explicit option rather than a required custodian[46]. This can reduce exposure to a provider-wide breach, not abolish exposure. Stolen devices, compromised updates, malicious documents and over-privileged agents remain threats; a shared software flaw can affect many local installations. Local tools need isolation, least privilege and explicit approval for sensitive actions[96]. Privacy is a property to test across the whole data path, not a label attached to where inference runs.

Local-first pays a second dividend: memory you can move. An assistant becomes harder to replace when useful context is trapped in its service. The Context Vault is our proposed response: an encrypted local store with documented export, per-client access grants and a readable audit trail. Retrieval would supply only the excerpts a task needs. If those excerpts are sent to a hosted model or a cloud-based client, they have left the local trust boundary; the interface must say so before the user agrees. Revoking future access cannot erase copies already received. The design is described on the Client page; it is not a shipped privacy guarantee.

Article IVThe Enki Index

Moore's law described a historical trend, not a promise. Inference prices are changing rapidly too: Stanford HAI measured a more than 280-fold decline at GPT-3.5-level performance from November 2022 to October 2024[13], while Epoch's 2025 analysis found large differences in price declines across tasks[14]. a16z's “LLMflation” describes the same competitive pressure[15]. Capability thresholds, benchmark choice and observation dates matter. A curve is not a covenant, and a manifesto cannot legislate one. What it can do is keep the receipts.

We propose the Enki Index: a public, quarterly series comparing hosted and self-hosted inference at fixed, disclosed capability targets. It is a research commitment, not an index already being published. Its methodology must record model and runtime versions, workload mix, accuracy, latency, energy, hardware amortisation and utilisation. Procurement should preserve the ability to benchmark, renegotiate and switch providers rather than assume an automatic annual price reduction. The falling price of useful work should benefit users, not disappear inside opaque contracts.

Article VOpen source is the commons of cognition

Open-weight models create a practical alternative to exclusive hosted access. DeepSeek reported competitive results for R1 against o1 on selected reasoning benchmarks[17]; the AI Index documents narrowing open-versus-closed gaps on some benchmarks, not universal parity[13]. But open weights are not automatically open source. The Open Source Initiative's definition also requires relevant code, data information and freedoms to use, study, modify and share; licences with use restrictions need to be identified honestly[95]. Benkler's account of commons-based peer production helps explain the value of shared infrastructure[67]. Our directory is a starting catalogue, not a certification scheme already run by 300 members. Its job is to make licences, deployment requirements and evidence easier to inspect. A download offers more control than an API alone; it does not establish safety, reproducibility or unrestricted rights.

Article VIThe local AI datacenter

The hardware for local intelligence is already present in phones and computers, though an NPU label alone does not establish model compatibility or sustained performance. Apple's published foundation-model work is one example of an on-device model alongside a larger server model[23]. Stanford and Together AI's Intelligence Per Watt study evaluated more than twenty local models, eight accelerators and one million single-turn chat and reasoning queries. Its 88.7% result describes queries successfully handled by at least one local model, not the guaranteed performance of one device or a deployed router; evaluation used automated judging and benchmark answers[25]. It does not establish 99% coverage of all human work, long-running agents, or every language. It does establish a substantial opportunity worth testing.

Now extend the idea: use the capable devices you own as a household pool, with a phone as the remote and a plugged-in computer as the sustained workhorse. Route a whole request to the machine able to run it. This is different from combining several machines to run one model. Memory capacity, bandwidth, runtime support, network latency and heat set real limits; owning the devices does not make those limits disappear.

Cross-device inference is a research path, not a feature we have shipped. Systems such as prima.cpp and Petals explore distributing model execution[80,85]. A trusted household reduces some coordination problems, but a home network is not automatically fast or secure. Our first objective is dependable discovery, authenticated enrolment and whole-request routing. Layer splitting must earn its place through measured benefits.

The proposed Enki device would make that local environment portable: small-model inference, owner-controlled updates and access for compatible clients. The hardware does not exist yet. We intend to publish buildable designs, seek funded distribution where possible and otherwise pursue a no-margin price; manufacturing, certification, power, support and repair still cost money. No manufacturer named on this website is an announced partner.

There is precedent for common infrastructure. Matter shows that competitors can agree on a connectivity standard, but also that certification and adoption do not eliminate interoperability problems[33,77]. The EU common-charger rules show a different tool: regulation can require compatible charging interfaces[78]. MCP's move to a vendor-neutral foundation offers an example of shared protocol custody[79]. None of these makes the Enki mesh automatic. Together they show that agreements, implementations and accountable stewardship can be built.

This is where we want more everyday AI to run: writing, translation, learning and selected local automations, with capability measured task by task. Web searches and external services still need network access, and high-stakes use needs appropriate validation and human judgment. Nor is local automatically greener or cheaper. The IPW study found that cloud accelerators could outperform local hardware on its efficiency measure when running identical models[25]. Reusing existing devices may avoid additional manufacturing, but savings depend on workload, idle power, lifetime and whether extra demand cancels them out. The Institute should publish lifecycle and total-cost comparisons rather than assume that every local token replaces a new datacenter. The ambition is affordable intelligence on hardware people control, with honest electricity, equipment and maintenance costs.

Article VIIThe standard we need

A useful local mesh needs a shared standard, not just a good demo. We propose four clauses. Model currency: signed releases, verified metadata, a public update record and a recovery policy. A signature establishes provenance and integrity, not that a model is harmless. Mesh enrolment: explicit owner consent, authenticated peers and capability declarations. Zero-configuration discovery can locate a device; it cannot authorise it.

Gateway recognition: separate inference APIs from MCP tools, with documented capabilities and per-client permissions. “Localhost” means the machine running the client; another device needs an authenticated LAN address or a local bridge. A cloud application cannot automatically reach a private home endpoint. Neutral custody: a proposed vendor-neutral steward, open conformance tests and a defensible certification process. These are design commitments, not an adopted international standard or an existing certification mark.

The building blocks are familiar. Content-addressed registries and formats such as GGUF make model distribution practical, but a file format does not make every model executable on every accelerator[83,84]. The catalogue must name the runtime, quantisation, memory and licence requirements. mDNS/DNS-SD can advertise a gateway[82]; authentication and enrolment decide who is allowed to use it. A proposed capability record must include measured sustained performance and changing resource availability, not just peak TOPS.

The gateway would route each request to an authorised, capable device and keep session state within the chosen trust boundary. Model files could be cached and shared among opted-in household peers. WAN mirrors require bandwidth, funding and licence permission; neither neighbourhood access nor unlimited redistribution can be assumed. Cross-device execution remains experimental rather than a fallback promised for every oversized model.

Update security must also be precise. TUF provides patterns for delegated signing and defence against stale metadata and rollback attacks[97]. An authorised recovery to an older model would require fresh, valid metadata, compatibility checks and a policy for what happens if no safe version remains. Binary deltas are an optimisation to measure: a new training run or quantisation can change much of the file. Security, key recovery, device support and conformance are substantial work. The standard must be engineered like we mean it, not declared finished because adjacent technologies exist.

Drafting, testing and defending this standard would be an early mandate of the Institute's technical and policy work. The open repository currently contains the proposal and engineering notes, not a complete interoperable implementation. The first compatibility claim must wait for a reproducible test.

Article VIIIRedirect the capital

We must redirect more capital and engineering away from a datacenter-only strategy and toward the technology people use every day. This is an investment demand: to fund the production of affordable, entry-level consumer graphics processors (GPUs), specialized AI processors (NPUs), affordable memory, and maintained open runtimes, available worldwide. It means building repairable hardware and ensuring fair distribution worldwide, not just in premium markets. The goal is to make local AI an accessible reality for everyone, and over time to extend it to suitable embedded appliances, heating and energy systems, powering a new generation of useful, intelligent devices.

This is not a call to abandon datacenters, which remain essential for training large models and handling intensive workloads. It is a call for balanced investment. There are concrete starting points, though none establishes an interoperable household mesh. Toolkits like Google AI Edge demonstrate how small language models can run on personal devices.[99] For narrower tasks, embedded processors like the STMicroelectronics STM32N6 target tasks such as vision and audio processing, with appliances and building controls among their intended applications.[100] In industrial settings, companies like Qarnot already reuse computing waste heat where it is needed.[101]

We propose a future where manufacturers of appliances, heating controls, water heaters, and solar or battery gateways can participate. A power connection or local energy source is not enough. Only where suitable, supported hardware exists could spare capacity run useful tasks, with owner consent. This requires memory, managed energy and thermal budgets, security updates, and strict isolation from safety-critical controls, always prioritizing the device’s primary function. We must allocate capital to build this resilient, owner-controlled foundation for everyday intelligence. Let’s fund the hardware that puts capable AI in the hands of the many, not only the few.

Article IXSilo the intelligence

The potential dangers of increasingly capable AI deserve serious governance, without treating every forecast as an observed catastrophe. Researchers have called mitigation of extinction-level AI risk a global priority[29]; work on biological misuse and international debate over autonomous weapons concern distinct, concrete risk pathways[30,31]. The International AI Safety Report reviews the evidence and its uncertainties[34]. Calls to prohibit superintelligence go further[35]. Enki's position is a proposal about how society should allocate and constrain power, not a claim that science has settled every risk.

We take a different position. We are not against superintelligence — we are against it sharing a nervous system with daily life. The answer is what engineers do with anything powerful and unproven: silo it. Three tiers, strictly separated.

At the top, frontier systems with potentially systemic capabilities: our proposal is declared infrastructure, independently evaluated containment and enforceable oversight. In the middle, compute-intensive research and industry: proportionate access controls, accountability and rules appropriate to the actual use. At the base, everyday AI: local-first models on supported personal or organisational hardware. These are proposed deployment tiers, not a complete scientific risk taxonomy. The EU AI Act's systemic-risk framework is a relevant precedent, but it does not establish Enki's proposed universal licensing or KYC/KYB scheme[37]. Capability, access and use matter across all three tiers.

The split is a policy choice, not a claim that 99% of every workload is already local-capable. Everyday tools should remain usable when a remote service is unavailable; the most consequential capabilities need proportionate oversight. Both goals require implementation, testing and public accountability.

Separation can improve containment and continuity: if a remote service is disconnected, independently operated local tools need not disappear with it. But a kill switch does not retrieve copied weights, undo actions already taken or guarantee control of a widely deployed system. California's debate over frontier-AI safety illustrates the difficulty of designing enforceable obligations[39]. Enki argues for tested isolation, incident reporting and recovery alongside local alternatives, not the comforting fiction that one plug makes frontier AI safe.

The limits of voluntary commitments are visible in the record. On 24 February 2026, Anthropic revised its Responsible Scaling Policy. TIME reported the removal of its earlier categorical commitment to pause when required safeguards were unavailable[87]. Anthropic's own explanation separates unilateral plans from industry-wide recommendations and adds public roadmaps, risk reports and external review in specified circumstances[88]. Calling that purely self-graded would omit material parts of the policy. Calling it enforceable public oversight would go too far in the other direction.

The more recent development is independent evaluation. In September, Anthropic announced an embedded-evaluation partnership led by Faculty, Accenture's AI business, funded directly by Anthropic, and discussions with METR and other nonprofit evaluators about self-funded pilots[93]. October reporting describes similar commitments from other labs and unresolved questions about funding, access and independence[94]. A commercial assessor, an independent nonprofit, a company-controlled foundation and a regulator are not interchangeable. The lesson for Enki is not that an NGO label solves governance. It is that scrutiny needs access, publication rights, disclosed conflicts and consequences. Strong oversight above and usable alternatives below reinforce each other; neither substitutes for the other.

This architecture also answers the loudest objection to everything we propose. When Meta declared open-source AI “the path forward” and shipped frontier-scale weights to the world[68], serious people called it a danger to humanity: U.S. senators demanded answers over the first Llama leak, warning of “the potential for its misuse in spam, fraud, malware, privacy violations, harassment, and other wrongdoing and harms”[69], and MIT biosecurity researchers cautioned that releasing the weights of future, more capable models could put pandemic-class agents within anyone's reach[70]. We take the objection seriously — and we answer it with architecture, not reassurance.

Small does not mean harmless. Risk depends on capabilities, data, tools, access and the setting in which a system operates, not parameter count alone. RAND's 2024 biological-risk study found no statistically significant uplift in its tested scenarios, while other reviews documented evidence gaps and recommended continued monitoring[71,72,73]. Those bounded results are not a safety certificate for later models. The EU's training-compute threshold is a presumption of systemic capability, not a safe harbour for everything below it; other models can also be designated[37]. We support useful local models with task-specific evaluations, least-privilege tools and meaningful human control. Public weights can widen access and scrutiny while also widening access for misuse. The commons needs stewardship, not innocence.

Frontier AI: independently scrutinised and contained. Research AI: accountable to its use. Everyday AI: local where practical, portable, and honestly costed.

Article XIt is not too late

Existing datacenters remain useful, and increasingly capable local models create another deployment option. What is missing is not permission to imagine it, but engineering, evidence and collective organisation. The device, standard, Index and Institute described here are work to be built, not accomplishments we should award ourselves in advance.

Nor is this argument new — it is one of the oldest in the philosophy of technology, and we are merely its latest signatories. Lewis Mumford warned in 1964 that every technics arrives in two forms: one authoritarian — system-centred, immensely powerful, inherently unstable — and one democratic — human-centred, modest, durable — and that a civilisation must actively choose between them[40]. Ivan Illich called a tool convivial when it extends human capability without making its users dependent on the institution that owns it[41]. E. F. Schumacher spent a whole book arguing for “technology with a human face” — production by the masses, not mass production[42]. Langdon Winner compressed the whole debate into four words — do artifacts have politics? — and answered yes: some technologies demand hierarchy simply to exist, while others invite self-government[66]. Replace “tool” with “model” and every one of those sentences lands, unchanged, in 2026.

Elinor Ostrom's work showed that user associations can govern common resources successfully and identified rules associated with those outcomes; it did not prove that commons always outperform markets or states[43]. That is the useful inheritance for the 300: clear responsibilities, contribution-based membership and accountable rules. At full membership, thirty seats would rotate annually through a defined peer-review process. The founder has no permanent entitlement: after the founding year Paul Fleury intends to step back into an honorary role without a reserved seat or special vote.

The same care is needed when reading the labs' governance. Under OpenAI's structure announced on 28 October 2025, the OpenAI Foundation retained control of OpenAI Group through special governance rights, despite holding a minority equity stake[89]. Ownership percentage is not control, and nonprofit control is not the same as independent external inspection. A legal form can support a public mission; only the rights, incentives and actual decisions show whether it does. Enki should be judged by that same standard.

The internet's end-to-end argument, CERN's release of the web and the local-first movement offer practical precedents for keeping capability and agency closer to users[44,45,46]. Everything this letter proposes belongs in that lineage. It must still earn its place there through working systems, open evidence and governance people can challenge.

So no — it is not too late, because “late” is the wrong axis entirely. The question was never when; it is where intelligence will live: in a few hundred warehouses, or in a few billion rooms. We do not fear machine intelligence. We fear a single point of failure wearing intelligence as a costume. The work of this generation is not to slow the machine down but to distribute it — to make intelligence like literacy: taught everywhere, owned by no one, impossible to repossess. A century from now, nobody will remember whose datacenter was tallest. They will ask only whether thinking became a utility bill or a birthright. That answer is still being written, and it is ours to write.

Paul Fleury

PostfaceThe oldest story we know

Why Enki? A name borrowed from stories about knowledge, power, and the choice to share.

Our name, Enki, is not decoration; it is a citation. The flood story it evokes is one of the oldest surviving narratives we use to think about hoarded intelligence. In the Sumerian original, the divine assembly votes to drown mankind, and though “the verdict, the word of the divine assembly, cannot be revoked”, Ziusudra is warned anyway, through a whisper addressed to the side-wall of his house[49]. In the Akkadian versions, the whisperer is Enki, bound by oath to tell no human. So he tells the architecture instead: “O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut! Wall, wall!” This is what we might now call a side-channel disclosure, the oath kept and betrayed in the same breath[50,51].

The flood was not Enki’s only leak. In Inana and Enki, the me, the arts of civilisation, from the craft of the scribe to the making of decisions, sail out of his city on the Boat of Heaven. Seven times the god of wisdom sends servants to seize the boat, seven times he fails, and the poem ends with the arts landing at Uruk, a city of people, to rejoicing at the Gate of Joy[52]. Assyriologists call Enki “the crafty god”, the one who repairs by wit what power breaks by decree[53].

These stories of Enki are not isolated; they echo across several traditions, depicting fire stolen, floods survived, and knowledge brought into human reach. Hesiod’s Prometheus steals fire, and Aeschylus associates him with human arts[54,55]. The Indian flood story of Manu and the biblical account of Noah concern warnings and survival[57,58]. Berossus describes Oannes teaching human arts[59]. Accounts of Raven and daylight, Māui and fire, Anansi and stories, and Quetzalcoatl and maize offer further images of precious things reaching people[61,62,63,64]. Frazer collected fire myths, while Kerényi interpreted Prometheus[60,65]. These are cultural analogies, not evidence that all societies share one political verdict. The traditions are not interchangeable, and no myth settles an engineering or policy question. They explain why we chose a name, borrowing a pattern, not a universal consensus.

Plato’s Phaedrus, however, gives the counterargument: Thamus worries that writing will offer the appearance of wisdom rather than its reality[56]. That caution belongs here too. Access to a powerful tool is not the same as learning to use it well. For Enki, wider access must come with that responsibility. When the assembly of gods resolved to flood mankind, Enki did not argue with the assembly. He warned one mortal and taught him to build a boat.

This letter is our warning. The 300 are our boatwrights. The local mesh is the hull.

The flood is optional. The boat is not.

Why “Enki”

Named after the god who sided with humanity.

The myth

He leaked the plan that saved us

Enki is the Sumerian god of wisdom, fresh water and craft — keeper of the me, the arts of civilisation. When the gods voted to drown humanity, Enki leaked the plan to a mortal, who built a boat. Civilisation survived because one intelligence refused to keep knowledge locked up above.

The mission

The name is the whole program

We are living the myth again: intelligence gathered into guarded warehouses, rented back by the token. Enki takes the god’s side — wisdom flows downward, like water. Open the models, put them on people’s devices, publish the true costs. Saving humanity, this time, means never depending on a single flood-proof ark.

Intelligence belongs to everyone. Everywhere.
